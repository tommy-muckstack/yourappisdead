Free alternatives to the SaaS tools most teams pay for

Updated October 26, 2026 · about 6 min read

Most small teams end up renting the same dozen tools: something for scheduling, something for docs, a task board, a design editor, a form builder, a way to sign contracts and send big files. Free and open-source replacements exist for almost all of them. This guide goes category by category and is honest about what you keep, what you give up, and when the paid product is still the better deal.

Start with the job, not the brand

Before you swap anything, write down what you actually use the tool for. A team paying for a full scheduling suite may only ever share one booking link. A team on a premium design plan may only export social graphics at two sizes. The narrower the job, the more likely a free tool covers it completely. The broader and more integrated the job (approvals, single sign-on, audit logs, dozens of connected apps), the more the paid product is doing for you behind the scenes.

A useful test: list the three features you would miss on day one if the tool vanished. If a free alternative covers those three, it is worth a trial. If it misses even one that touches customers or money, keep paying until it doesn't.

Category by category

Scheduling

Booking links are the classic "pay every month for one feature" tool. CalendlyIsDead covers event types, calendar sync with Google, Outlook and iCloud, team round robin, reminders and paid bookings through your own Stripe account. What you give up is the zero-setup experience: calendar sync needs your own OAuth clients, and confirmation emails need an email-sending account. If you never want to think about deliverability or API keys, a paid scheduler earns its fee.

Docs, wikis and databases

NotionIsDead gives you nested pages, a block editor with Markdown shortcuts, backlinks and Markdown import and export. AirtableIsDead covers typed grid fields, saved grid and kanban views, filters, and CSV in and out. Both currently keep data in your browser, so there is no real-time collaboration or cross-device sync yet, and the Airtable clone has no linked records, formulas or automations. For a personal wiki or a single-owner tracker that is fine. For a shared company knowledge base, the paid tools are still ahead.

Project tracking

For boards, TrelloIsDead is one of the most complete apps in the collection: workspaces, roles, invites, checklists, due dates, several views and simple automation rules, with live updates between collaborators. For engineering-style issue tracking, LinearIsDead offers keyboard-first issues, cycles, projects and a command menu, but stores workspaces in the browser and has no GitHub or Slack integrations. If your workflow depends on issues closing automatically when code merges, that integration is worth paying for.

Whiteboards and design

MiroIsDead provides an infinite canvas with stickies, shapes, connectors, frames and live cursors between collaborators. CanvaIsDead is a drag-and-drop editor with multi-page designs and local PNG, JPG, SVG and PDF export without a watermark. The big gap on the design side is content: there is no stock photo library, brand kit or template marketplace. If your team leans on a large licensed asset library, that library is most of what you are paying for.

Forms and surveys

TypeformIsDead does one-question-at-a-time forms with logic jumps, an NPS template, per-question charts and CSV export. It does not yet do file uploads, payments, webhooks or custom themes. For feedback surveys and RSVPs it is plenty; for a lead form wired into a CRM, check that the integration you need exists first.

E-signature

DocuSignIsDead lets you upload a PDF, place signature, date and text fields, route it to several recipients in order, and download a flattened PDF with an audit certificate. It provides simple electronic signatures only: no independent identity verification and no advanced or qualified signatures. For routine agreements between parties who know each other, that is often enough. For regulated industries or contracts where identity proof matters, a paid provider with those guarantees is the right call.

File transfer

WeTransferIsDead sends files with a link, an expiry, an optional password and a delete key, with no signup. Be aware of the limits: the hosted version accepts only small uploads because of serverless request size caps, and a self-hosted build allows 100 MB per transfer, far below what paid transfer services offer. There is also no virus scanning. If you routinely send multi-gigabyte video, keep a paid plan or use storage you already pay for.

Passwords

1PasswordIsDead encrypts a vault in your browser with a master password, and includes a generator, one-time codes and a security audit. Its own documentation says it is experimental, not independently audited, and has no sync, no browser extension and no account recovery. Treat it as something to read and learn from, not the only home for your important credentials. If you want a free, open-source password manager for daily use, choose a mature project with a long track record, or keep paying for an established product. This is the category where saving money is least worth the risk.

Meeting notes and transcription

GranolaIsDead is a Mac app that records your microphone and system audio, transcribes on the device and turns your notes into structured summaries, without a bot joining the call. OtterIsDead runs in the browser using the browser's own speech recognition, with transcripts you can edit and export. You give up team sharing, cloud sync and, in the browser version, automatic speaker detection. Whatever you use, get consent before recording anyone.

Screen recording, screenshots and dictation

These are Mac apps rather than web apps. LoomIsDead records screen and camera and uploads to a watch page with reactions and comments; links are unlisted rather than private, and there are no auto-transcripts. CleanShotXIsDead covers area, window and scrolling capture, annotation and screen recording. WisprFlowIsDead offers hold-to-talk dictation that runs on the device. All three need macOS 14.2 or later, and none has a Windows version.

Email, writing and links

SuperhumanIsDead is a keyboard-first Gmail client for the Mac. You bring your own Google OAuth client, and optionally an AI API key, so setup takes a few minutes and there is no support desk. GrammarlyIsDead checks spelling, grammar and clarity in the browser using rules and a dictionary, with no generative rewrites and US English only. BitlyIsDead and LinktreeIsDead cover short links, QR codes and link-in-bio pages with click counts; using your own short domain requires self-hosting.

What "free" really costs

Free software still costs something, just not a monthly invoice. Expect to pay in three currencies:

  • Setup time. Web apps that you use on yourappisdead.com need no setup, but the moment you want your own domain, your own email sender or your own storage, you are self-hosting. Our guide on self-hosting an open-source SaaS app walks through what that involves.
  • Support. There is no account manager. Help comes from the README, the issue tracker and your own troubleshooting.
  • Maturity. Many of these apps are new. Several keep data only in your browser, which means clearing site data can delete it. Export regularly.

When paying is still the right call

Paid SaaS is often good value, and it is fair to say so. Keep paying when:

  • The tool holds data you are legally required to retain, protect or audit.
  • You need single sign-on, admin controls, or a security review your customers ask for.
  • A free tool would save less money than the hours it takes your team to switch and maintain it.
  • The paid tool's integrations do real work, such as syncing to your CRM or closing issues from commits.
  • Losing access for a day would hurt customers.

A sensible approach is to move the low-risk, single-purpose tools first (link pages, short links, screenshots, simple forms), see how it goes, and leave the critical ones for later.
